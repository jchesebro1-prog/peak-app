Assessment and Rigging Intake Alignment Brief
Oct 13, 2026 · @Jeff Chesebro
Bottom line
Tomorrow's checklist covers the general theatre survey well but captures too little rigging data to hand off cleanly. Add short rigging triage and power and control blocks, use one ID per finding, and carry the same rating words through every stage. That lets each finding be followed from your site visit to a rigging inspection, a quote, repair and re-inspection.
This brief draws on your eight sample reports (Warsaw, Florida Southern, St. Norbert, Iowa, Zion-Benton, Woodstock Opera House, Rochester Civic and UWSP) and the CESA 10 Manitowoc audit. It assumes your assessment screens and flags rigging, and an ETCP-certified rigger performs the actual inspection. Correct that if you plan to inspect rigging yourself.

Rating scales
The rigging reports use at least four different scales, and the CESA 10 audit uses a fifth, point-based one. Use one three-level rating on every finding from site visit to close-out, and record the audit's scores beside it, so findings sort the same way in every document.

Three notes keep the scales honest. First, ACD's High, Medium, Low and the Florida Southern budget tiers ($, $$, $$$) measure priority and cost, not condition, so track them in separate columns, not in the rating. Second, treat any fall hazard, fire curtain or smoke vent failure, or unrated overhead hardware as Immediate Attention regardless of cost; the Lincoln catwalk gaps are one example. Third, the audit's cost tier (under 10,000 dollars, under 100,000 dollars, over 100,000 dollars) can carry over unchanged as the budget column.
Tracking chain
Give every finding one ID at the site visit and keep it unchanged through every later stage. Use the pattern district, building, system, sequence; for example MPSD-LHS-RIG-003 for the third rigging finding at Lincoln High School. Where a finding matches an item in the CESA 10 audit, record that audit item beside it, such as the Lincoln catwalk gap item, so the two records stay linked.
	•	Intake: the site visit records the finding, its rating, photo numbers and location, using the fields above.
	•	Triage: each finding is tagged theatrical-general, rigging or electrical and control; rigging items go to an ETCP-certified rigger and electrical items to a qualified electrician.
	•	Rigging inspection: the inspector adds component-level conditions, line set numbers and standards cited, and may reclassify the rating. Both ratings stay on record.
	•	Recommendation: each item gets an action, a priority, a budget tier and an owner, either staff or rigging contractor, as in the Warsaw summary table.
	•	Quote or work order: the contractor's quote references the finding ID.
	•	Repair: record the date, who did the work, and the parts or hardware used, with before and after photos.
	•	Verification: a re-inspection or sign-off confirms the fix and closes the ID.
	•	Ongoing logs: closed items feed the maintenance and inspection log and the fire curtain test log, with a next-due date. The Warsaw report recommends fire curtain tests every 90 days and treats the ANSI E1.47 chart as the guide for inspection frequency.

Reference documents: ANSI E1.47 and the manufacturer guides
I read ANSI E1.47 (the rigging inspection standard), the Wenger rigging guide and the J.R. Clancy design guide through a web summary, not page by page. Check the figures against the source documents before quoting any of them. The E1.47 summary labeled itself the 2017 edition although the link name says 2014, and it flagged its own motorized frequency row as ambiguous. E1.47 excludes building structure and building electrical, so our assessment should flag those for an engineer or electrician and not rate them.
